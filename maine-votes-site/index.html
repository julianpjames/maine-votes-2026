Reproductive rights: beyond the sound bites

A documented comparison of Susan Collins and Troy Jackson. We distinguish candidates’ stated positions, recorded legislative actions, and the consequences of judicial decisions. Records span different levels of government and are not identical policy tests.

Evidence standard: The dated votes and bill texts below link to primary sources. Candidate explanations are attributed to the candidate. This is a selected record, not an exhaustive study of either career.
Republican · U.S. senator

Susan Collins

Collins says she supports statutory protections similar to those in Roe v. Wade and Planned Parenthood v. Casey. In February 2022 she introduced the Reproductive Choice Act with Sen. Lisa Murkowski; in August she joined the Reproductive Freedom for All Act, which also addressed contraception access.

She opposed the Women’s Health Protection Act, arguing that it reached beyond restoring Roe and could displace existing laws and conscience protections. She voted against advancing H.R. 3755 on February 28, 2022.

Democrat · Former Maine Senate president

Troy Jackson

Jackson voted to enact LD 1619 in July 2023. The law allows abortion after viability when a licensed physician, applying the relevant medical standard of care, determines the procedure is necessary. That replaced the narrower post-viability statutory conditions that had applied previously.

The recorded vote establishes his support for the enacted Maine measure; it does not by itself establish his position on every possible federal abortion bill.

Evidence timeline

Oct. 6, 2018

Collins votes to confirm Brett Kavanaugh

Collins voted Yea on the Supreme Court nomination. The Senate confirmed him 50–48 (one senator voted “Present”). Kavanaugh later joined the majority in Dobbs, which overturned Roe. A confirmation vote is not a vote on a particular future decision, but it is a consequential, documented action.

Official Senate confirmation roll call ↗
Feb. 28, 2022

Different approaches to federal abortion legislation

Collins and Murkowski introduced the Reproductive Choice Act. That same day, Collins voted Nay on cloture for the motion to proceed to the Women’s Health Protection Act (H.R. 3755). The 46–48 cloture result meant the chamber did not advance the bill on that motion; it was not a final-passage vote.

June 24, 2022

Dobbs overturns Roe; Collins criticizes ruling

Following the Supreme Court ruling, Collins called overturning the longstanding precedent a destabilizing decision and argued that a baseline federal protection should remain. The public statement provides her response to a ruling joined by a justice she voted to confirm.

Collins’s full statement ↗
Aug. 1, 2022

Collins joins another federal codification proposal

Collins joined Sens. Tim Kaine, Lisa Murkowski, and Kyrsten Sinema in introducing the Reproductive Freedom for All Act. Its stated purpose was to establish federal abortion protections and protect contraception access. Introduction is distinct from enactment.

Proposal announcement ↗
July 6, 2023

Jackson votes for Maine LD 1619

Jackson voted Yea on enactment; the Maine Senate passed it 20–11 with four excused. The bill became law on July 19, 2023. Maine’s current statute uses a licensed physician’s professional judgment and the applicable standard of care for abortions after viability.

Questions a voter may reasonably ask

What is the difference between Collins’s proposal and the Women’s Health Protection Act?

Collins’s February 2022 proposal aimed to write the central Roe and Casey framework into federal statute. She argued that the broader Women’s Health Protection Act would override other longstanding statutory protections, including conscience protections. Understanding whether that distinction is persuasive requires reading both bills’ actual provisions—not simply assigning a “for” or “against” label based on one procedural vote.

Collins’s stated objections ↗

Does Maine’s law allow abortion after viability without restrictions?

No. Title 22 §1598 says an abortion after viability may be performed only when a licensed physician determines it is necessary in the physician’s professional judgment, using the applicable standard of care. Debate over how broad that clinical standard is should not be conflated with the absence of any legal condition.

Read the statute ↗

Can Collins’s and Jackson’s legislative votes be compared directly?

Only with context. Collins voted on whether the U.S. Senate should proceed to debate a particular federal bill; Jackson voted on final enactment of a Maine law. The proposals addressed different legal questions and were subject to different legislative procedures. Both votes illuminate policy choices, but they are not identical tests.

Accountability case · documented, not a fact-check rating

Collins’s Kavanaugh vote and the overturning of Roe

The documented sequence is: Collins voted to confirm Justice Kavanaugh in 2018; he later joined the majority overturning Roe in 2022; Collins publicly criticized the decision. Readers can examine the confirmation vote and her reaction side by side. This evidence alone does not prove what she knew Kavanaugh would decide in the future.

Healthcare research · October 8, 2026

Healthcare: coverage, costs, and rural access

A closer look at the records behind the promises. Federal and Maine legislative actions operate at different levels; the guide distinguishes proposals, procedural votes, final votes and enacted outcomes.

Susan Collins · Republican

Targeted reforms and Medicaid protections

Collins has advocated specific bipartisan drug-pricing measures, including work with Sen. Jeanne Shaheen on insulin costs. In July 2025 she voted against final passage of the federal reconciliation bill, identifying Medicaid cuts and risks to Maine’s rural hospitals as principal objections. She also described support for certain work requirements.

Troy Jackson · Democrat

System-wide coverage and drug affordability

Jackson’s current U.S. Senate campaign advocates Medicare for All and lower prescription-drug prices. As a Maine state senator he supported MaineCare expansion proposals and sponsored LD 2114, a 2024 measure requiring information on health insurers’ coverage of generic medicines and biosimilars. His earlier gubernatorial campaign discussed a Maine public option and rural healthcare investments.

Examine the evidence

2025 · Did Collins vote for the Medicaid cuts?

Collins voted to advance consideration of the federal reconciliation package, then voted against its final passage. She specifically cited Medicaid effects in explaining her final vote. Saying she voted for final enactment of the cuts omits a critical distinction; saying she had no role in advancing the bill also omits the procedural vote.

Independent analysis of both votes ↗ · Collins’s explanation ↗

Medicaid expansion · What did Jackson accomplish?

Jackson supported Maine legislative attempts to expand MaineCare, but those efforts were blocked under Governor Paul LePage. Maine voters approved expansion in 2017; the expansion was implemented in 2019 under Governor Janet Mills. Jackson contributed to advocacy and legislative efforts but cannot be credited alone with implementation.

Detailed account and sources ↗

Prescription drugs · What can we actually document?

Collins and Democratic colleagues proposed insulin supply-chain and cost-sharing measures in 2022. Jackson sponsored LD 2114 to improve information on how health insurers cover generics and biosimilars in 2024. Those are different interventions; neither should be characterized as direct proof of lower prices without outcome data.

Follow the moneyCampaign receipts, expenditures and original filings
The money trail · Public disclosures

Follow the money.

Candidate fundraising is only part of the picture. Explore reported campaign receipts and spending, then look separately at independent political advertising. Every figure should be read with its reporting period.

How to read the numbers

Receipts are not the same as individual donations

Campaign receipts can include individual and committee contributions, transfers, and other permitted categories. Adding them all to an “individual donations” total would be misleading.

Outside spending is not a contribution to a campaign

Independent expenditures are made without coordination with a candidate or campaign, and should be reported separately from candidate committee spending. A group spending on ads is not necessarily the same thing as a candidate receiving that money.

Why aren't all races showing totals yet?

We publish a number only after matching the right candidate and authorized committees, date window, transaction categories, and original filing. Maine state and federal reporting systems use different datasets and filing schedules.
